*Companion piece to [HHS Allows Temporary Compounding of Six Unapproved Peptides](https://medhane-hagos-mesgena.rainymoon131.chatgpt.site/articles/hhs-allows-temporary-compounding-of-six-unapproved-peptides).*

Within a day of the HHS announcement I started getting the question in clinic: "So BPC-157 is legal now?" The honest
answer is that the legal picture changed and the medical picture did not. Here is how I explain the difference.

## What changed on October 28

HHS released an interim policy, scheduled for the October 30 Federal Register, saying it will exercise "enforcement
discretion" toward state-licensed pharmacies, federal facilities and licensed physicians who compound six peptides:
BPC-157, TB-500, Semax, Epitalon, KPV and MOTS-c. The policy is a bridge while FDA writes a formal rule.

Compounders have to meet conditions. The peptide must match the identity described in the policy and come from an
FDA-registered manufacturer following current good manufacturing practice. Every batch needs a certificate of analysis
covering identity, purity, potency and contaminants. Pharmacies must report what they compound, any serious adverse
events and any quality problems to FDA's MedWatch program and to their state boards. Routes are restricted by peptide;
Semax, for example, is limited to intranasal use.

## What did not change

HHS says plainly that the policy does not establish that these products are safe or effective. None of the six has
been through an FDA approval trial. Reporting indicates FDA's own reviewers concluded the evidence for each was
insufficient, even though an advisory panel recommended them in July. "Allowed to be compounded" is a regulatory status,
not a clinical endorsement.

That matters for patients because nearly everything written online about these peptides comes from animal studies,
small uncontrolled human series or marketing copy. For BPC-157 and TB-500 in particular, the human data on dosing,
duration, interactions and long-term effects are thin.

## What I tell patients who want to try one

1. **Ask where it comes from.** Under the policy, a legitimate compounded product traces to an FDA-registered
   manufacturer with a batch certificate of analysis. If the pharmacy cannot show you that, it is not operating under
   this policy and the "research use only" vials sold online never were.
2. **Ask what it is supposed to do for you, specifically.** "Healing" and "recovery" are not endpoints. If the goal is
   a tendon injury, we should have an exam, often imaging and a plan with physical therapy first, because that is
   what the evidence supports.
3. **Tell every clinician you see that you are taking it.** These peptides are not in most drug-interaction databases.
   Your surgeon, your cardiologist and your pharmacist need to know.
4. **Report side effects.** The policy depends on adverse-event reporting to MedWatch. If you have a reaction, tell your
   prescriber and ask that it be reported. That is how the formal rule will be informed.
5. **Expect the rules to move.** Companies with the most to gain, including Hims & Hers, have said they are waiting for
   final FDA rules before selling. If the biggest commercial players are waiting, a cautious patient can too.

## What to watch

The Federal Register publication on October 30 will carry the exact conditions and dose limits. The next milestone is
FDA's proposed rule, which has no announced date. I will update this piece when it is published.
